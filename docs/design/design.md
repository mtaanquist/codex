# Codex - design

A writing workspace for long-form creative work - novels, serials, campaigns, worldbuilding projects that grow across years. Runs in Docker, accessed through a browser. Two ways to use it: install it on a server you control, or sign up on the hosted service run by the project maintainer. Same code either way, same features, MIT-licensed. There is no feature-gated tier; the difference between the two paths is who handles backups, TLS, and updates.

Companion documents:
- `schema.md` - full Postgres schema.
- `roadmap.md` - phased build sequence.

---

## What Codex is

Codex sits between the formal tools that treat writing as a deliverable (Scrivener, Final Draft) and the prose editors that treat writing as a single document (markdown editors, Word). What it adds is structure: a **universe** that holds shared worldbuilding, **stories** that tell specific narratives within that universe, **chapters** and **scenes** that carry the prose, and **characters, places, and lore entries** - cross-referenced, searchable, visible alongside the writing surface without crowding it.

It is not a chat tool. It is not a role-play tool. It is not, principally, a publishing platform - though it exports what you need to take your work elsewhere. The shape is the same whether you self-host or use the hosted service: one shared instance with a number of accounts on it, each user's work scoped to them and private by default, and published pages reachable by anyone (the GitHub model). What differs between self-host and hosted is only who is responsible for the box it runs on, not the model.

## Who uses it

Three types of user shape the product, and in practice they overlap - most people using Codex will be some combination of all three at different moments.

**The novelist** writes long-form fiction, sometimes standalone, often series. They care about prose quality, consistent worldbuilding across books, finding every mention of a character to verify a detail, and recovering a draft from a week ago because the restructure didn't actually work. They protect their flow state and bristle at anything that interrupts it.

**The worldbuilder** may or may not be writing a specific story. What they're building is the world itself - its geography, its histories, its factions, its magic or its science. They want to hold their material in structured form and link it together. For them the stories (if any) are a way to explore the world; the world is the primary thing.

**The TTRPG designer or DM** is running a campaign, writing a setting book, or both. They share a lot with the worldbuilder and some with the novelist - session recaps, NPC journals, faction politics, encounter notes. They want to record canon without being forced into a novel-shaped "story" structure.

Codex does not ask you to pick a lane. It gives you primitives and lets you use them however fits. A novelist's *series bible* and a DM's *campaign setting* map cleanly onto the same concept - a universe with characters, places, and lore, and any number of story-shaped containers drawing from it.

## What it feels like to use

### Starting fresh

You land on your library. It shows the universes you've already created - "The shattered realms," "Tenfold moon" - each with its cluster of stories, plus any standalones. There is a dashed-border button at the top right: *New universe*. You click it, name it *The long cold*, add three sentences of description, and create your first story inside it. An empty scene is already waiting when you land in the writing view: status *outline*, position zero, untitled. You start typing.

### Drafting

Forty minutes in. The editor has underlined - in quiet category colours, dotted not solid - the names of characters and places you've introduced earlier. The first time you type "Hal" in this scene, a faded "den" appears after your cursor; Tab makes it "Halden." An hour later you decide to restructure the opening. Before you rewrite, you open the History tab in the right sidebar and add a manual checkpoint called "before restructure." You rewrite. Two hours after that, you realise the original was actually better. You open the History tab again, click the "before restructure" entry, see the earlier version in the centre column with a blue *Viewing a past revision* banner, and click *Restore*. Nothing is lost; your restructured attempt becomes a new revision in the timeline, still recoverable.

### Coming back

Three weeks later you open Codex. Your library's *Recent* strip shows *Book of ash* at the top - "edited 3 weeks ago." You click in. The scene you were working on is still open. The right sidebar panels you had pinned are still pinned. Your entity underlines are still there. You pick up without orientation.

### Stretching across books

A year later you start *The northern march*, book two in the same universe. You add it as a second story under *The shattered realms*. Alice, Halden, the treaty - all already exist; you don't re-describe them. But in this new book, Alice is seven years older and living somewhere else. You open her entity page; in the *In this book* section (story-scoped, visually distinct with a left accent) you write what's changed for her. Book one's notes are untouched.

### Just writing

The writing experience stands on its own. You sit down to write and the tool gives you a focused editor with your worldbuilding within arm's reach. Nothing appears unbidden: entity autocomplete is local and lookup-based, and the Assistant (the LLM layer, see "AI: the Assistant" below) is off by default, opt-in per account with a per-story mute. A user who keeps it off sees no AI UI anywhere. The ordering was deliberate: v1 shipped with no AI at all, the editor had to be good on its own merits first, and the prose corpus from real use became the calibration material for the assistance that arrived later.

### Turning over a universe

Years in, your universe has grown large enough that you want to share it. You open universe settings, go to Export, and download a markdown archive - a zip of plain markdown files, each with YAML front matter, organised into folders for characters, places, lore, and a subfolder per story. The archive opens cleanly in Obsidian. You hand it to a friend, who reads through it without needing to run Codex at all. Your words have never been trapped.

## Product structure

```
universe
  |-- story (one or more)
  |     `-- chapter (organisational container)
  |           `-- scene (atomic writing unit)
  `-- characters, places, lore entries (universe-scoped; surfaced per story by reference)
```

A few decisions this shape encodes:

**Universe, not "book" or "series.**" The container holds shared worldbuilding, and books or series are subsets of that. A single novel has a universe of its own, implicitly. A series has one universe with multiple stories. A TTRPG setting is a universe with no stories at all, or with an ongoing "campaign" story that accumulates session notes.

**Story, not "novel.**" Form-agnostic. Novels, novellas, serials, short stories, and campaigns all fit.

**Scene, not chapter, is the atomic writing unit.** Scenes own prose. Chapters are organisational. A scene has POV, location, time, status, word count, and an optional summary. Chapters are parents that can be reordered and summarised. This is Scrivener's instinct and it's correct - scenes are the unit of writing.

**Orphan scenes are allowed.** A scene's `chapter_id` is nullable, so you can draft before committing to chapter structure.

**Characters, places, and lore are universe-scoped, surfaced per story by reference.** Alice-at-32 and Alice-at-39 are the same character record, with per-book notes layered on top. Identity lives on the universe; context lives on the story. A story does not own its own entities: its Plan view shows the universe entities it declares as members or mentions in prose, and creating an entity while inside a story simply adds it to the universe and marks it a member of that story.

**Lore categories are user-defined.** Every universe starts with a single "Lore" category; users rename it, delete it, and add as many more as they need - "Factions," "Rituals," "Technologies," "Historical events." Characters and places stay first-class because they have behaviours (aliases, mention detection, POV assignment) the generic lore shape doesn't need.

## The writing surface

The editor exists at two scopes: **story** and **universe**. Both use the same three-column shell - structure on the left, working area in the centre, reference on the right - and the same underlying components. What differs is which views are available and what the components display.

**Inside a story**, the editor offers three views, selected by tabs at the top of the left sidebar:

- **Write** shows the manuscript tree (chapters and scenes) on the left, a markdown editor on the centre column rendered in Literata, and reference panels on the right (pinned entities, universe summary, category catalogues, mentions in this scene).
- **Plan** swaps the left sidebar for a list of the story's entities (the universe entities it uses, grouped by category) and the centre column for an entity editor with fields for summary, body, aliases, story-specific notes, and relationships. Right sidebar shows mentions and relations.
- **Notes** shows freeform notes grouped by pinned and recent, with the same prose-first editor in the centre. Notes can be scoped to the universe, the story, or a specific scene.

**Inside a universe**, the editor still shows all four modes, because the strip is the same four everywhere; **Plan** and **Notes** open at universe scope, while Write and Review resolve to the universe's story list, since both need a story open. One line under the strip says so. A universe doesn't contain prose directly; it contains the worldbuilding that stories draw on. The universe-level Plan is the same entity editor component as the story-level Plan, except it shows every entity in the universe (rather than the subset a story uses) and hides the *In this book* story-scoped section (there's no story context at this scope to define "in this book" against). The universe-level Notes is the same notes component, filtered to notes that aren't attached to any specific story.

This structure - universes as first-class editable surfaces, not just settings containers - is important for worldbuilders and TTRPG designers who may never write a prose "story" but need a full editor for characters, places, and lore. It's also important for novelists adding a character who'll cross multiple books: the natural home for such a character is the universe, not whichever book they happened to appear in first.

The left strip is routes and never changes shape; the right pane is panels about whatever the centre holds, so it changes with the mode. There are five panels, one order, one label each: **Reference** (what the prose or entry mentions), **Comments** (comments and suggestions on this text), **Assistant** (the text read against the story's settings), **History** (versions of what you are editing), and **Notes** (notes on this scene). A panel with no subject on a page is hidden rather than disabled or renamed, because a subject is not something a viewer can be granted; where one panel is left the pane wears that panel's title instead of a one-segment control, and where none is left the pane closes. The strip is a real tablist, so the panels are reachable by arrow keys and announced as tabs. The Assistant panel shows only when the account has the Assistant configured and enabled and the story has not muted it (see `assistant.md` and "AI: the Assistant" below). A command palette (Ctrl+K), which absorbs search and cross-view navigation into one surface, was added later in Phase 7; it is a shortcut to the navigation model, never a substitute for it, so anything reachable only from the palette is a bug. See `design-system.md` for the panel-by-page table and the primitives.

Every page, at either scope, wears one navigation bar: brand, then the path (Library / Universe / Story / Page), then the tools. A crumb opens that place's home, and a place in the path is a menu whose first item goes to the place itself and whose remaining items are that place's own things, settings included. There is no gear and no icon-only destination. Help is a page in that same chrome, reached from the question mark in the tools, which lights up while you are there and takes you back when pressed again; it never opens over the top of your work. See `design-system.md` for the full chrome contract.

### Where the working preferences went

An earlier sketch gave the right sidebar a fourth tab called *Session*: the preferences that change mid-sitting (entity autocomplete, entity underlines, theme, focus mode) plus the day's word count, the daily goal bar and the streak card. The surfaces pass removed it. "Session" named the sitting rather than anything in the pane, and the panel it sat in was not about the document the other panels were about. The working preferences live in the editor's own toolbar and view menu and on the account page; the numbers live in the **Writing sessions** section of a universe's Insights page, universe-scoped, where the rest of the counting already was. The word *Session* does not appear in the product; the chat surface is called **Assistant** everywhere, and the per-story mute lives with that panel.

### Entity mentions

The feature that ties planning and writing together. Each scene is scanned on debounced save for known entity names and aliases. Matches are stored in an index table and rendered live in the CodeMirror editor as dotted underlines in the entity's category colour. Hovering shows a short popover with the entity's summary; clicking opens it in the reference panel or jumps to its entry.

A per-entity `auto_detect_mentions` flag disables matching for common-word names ("Will," "Art," "Rose") where you don't want false positives. Aliases are first-class: Mrs. Fenwick and Alice are the same person, and both trigger the underline.

### Entity relationships

Where mentions are *implicit* links derived from prose, relationships are **explicit** links declared by the user. A character is the parent of another character; a character lives in a place; a place is part of a larger place. Relationships are the worldbuilding equivalent of mentions: they cross-reference entities, but the user enters them directly rather than the system discovering them.

Each relationship has a typed label from a defined set - *parent of, sibling of, rival of, lives in, rules, part of,* and so on - plus optional free-text notes. The relation types ship with sensible built-in defaults (family, social, geography) and can be extended per-universe; a universe about sworn-sword hierarchies or starship crews can add the types that fit it without the tool privileging any particular genre. Direction matters: *parent of* is asymmetric, rendered as "child of" on the target's page; *sibling of* is symmetric, rendered the same on both sides. The free-text label is never user-typed - it's always picked from the defined set, so relationships stay filterable, inverse-renderable, and graphable (the relationship web view shipped in v2.16.0).

Relationships can be scoped to a specific story, so a reconciliation in book two doesn't have to rewrite the universe-level rival relationship that's true across the broader canon. Story-scoped relationships supersede universe-wide ones on that story's pages.

The entity editor shows relationships as a dedicated section below the Description. The Reference panel in the right sidebar shows them alongside the mention-derived "Related" list - relationships in one group, mention co-occurrences in another, visually distinct so the user can tell which links are authored and which are discovered.

### Entity autocomplete

Building on the mention index, the editor also offers completion suggestions while you type. The behaviour is user-configurable through a three-option preference: **off**, **inline ghost-text**, or **popup menu**. Ghost-text is the recommended default for prose - the rest of "Halden" appears as faded text after your cursor when you type "Hal," and Tab accepts it. The popup is a dropdown showing multiple matches with arrow-key selection, more familiar to anyone coming from IDEs. Off disables the feature without affecting the underlines. The three options are exposed because writers genuinely disagree on the best shape, and CodeMirror's compartment-based configuration makes them cheap to switch between.

### History

Every debounced save produces a revision. Manual checkpoints can be added at any time with a short label. Revisions are polymorphic across every editable entity - scenes, chapters, characters, places, lore entries, notes - and surface at three scopes:

- **Per-item** via the History tab in the right sidebar: shows the timeline for whatever is currently open.
- **Per-story** via the History section of story settings: every revision in every scene, chapter, and story-scoped note, across time.
- **Per-universe** via the History section of universe settings: broadens the same lens to every change anywhere in the universe, including worldbuilding entities.

Every revision is kept indefinitely; consecutive autosaves within two minutes coalesce into one row. A selected revision can be previewed in the centre column (with a clear banner indicating preview mode) or restored, which, crucially, creates a new revision on top of the current one rather than overwriting it. Restoration is therefore reversible.

A diff view sits alongside the timeline. Any two revisions can be compared, and a previewed revision is shown as a diff against the current text by default, with insertions and deletions marked inline. It is the same diff component wherever changes need to be shown, including a reviewer's suggested edits (see the review feature in `schema.md`), so it is built once and reused.

### Saved drafts

A saved draft is a named copy of a whole story at one moment, the way a tag marks a commit in a git repository: "this is the first draft, and I always want to be able to go back to it". It is private, and separate from publishing (a published edition is for readers; a saved draft is for the writer). Shipped 2026-11-16 (#812).

- **A total freeze.** A draft keeps the chapters and scenes (text, summaries, status), the story's notes, and the universe's characters, places, and lore with their categories, details, relationships, and this story's notes on them. It is stored as one self-contained snapshot (`story_drafts.content`), not as references to revisions, so nothing that later happens to the live rows or to History can change or break it. Only its name and note can be edited; a writer can delete it outright.
- **Switching is reading.** Opening a saved draft shows it read-only, with a banner, on its own page; nothing on that page writes over the working draft. From a draft the writer can compare the whole story with the working draft or another saved draft (the Compare tab: scenes matched by id, or by `scenes.metadata.fromDraftScene` for one added back, older to newer), compare a scene with the working draft, restore one scene (the replaced text is kept in that scene's History first, so it can be undone), add back a scene deleted since, start a new story from the draft, or publish the draft. There is deliberately no whole-story restore over the working draft: "new story from this draft" is the safe way back.
- **Where it shows.** A drafts crumb after the story's title on Write and on the draft page, a "Drafts" menu on the story's library cover once it has a draft, and a Saved drafts section in story settings for saving, renaming, and deleting. Markdown exports of the story, its universe, and the account carry each draft in its own folder.

### Focus mode

Focus mode, chosen from the View menu on the editor's formatting bar, hides the navigation bar and both sidebars, using `visibility: hidden` on the sidebars rather than `display: none`, so the prose column stays anchored where it was instead of shifting to the new centre of a now-wider grid. The mode doesn't widen the prose - it removes the chrome around it. The content width setting controls how wide the prose column actually is, independently of focus.

### Continuous view

The Write view can also show the whole story as one continuous document: scenes flow together in story order, chapter titles act as section headings, and the sidebar becomes navigation, scrolling to whatever it names. The point is reading and revising a draft the way a reader will meet it. The flow is editable in place (shipped as step 23b): each scene is its own stitched editor autosaving separately, one formatting bar acts on whichever editor is focused, and the caret crosses scene boundaries with the arrow keys. A scene's editor mounts as it nears the visible part of the document (its text shows as plain lines until then), and every editor shares one compiled mention matcher, so a long novel does not open hundreds of editors at once. Whether scene marks render inside the flow is a preference, set at the account level with a per-story override and defaulting to shown; some authors treat scenes as atomic splits that should not interrupt the reading flow. The rendering is driven by `global_position`, so reordering scenes reorders the reading flow.

### Images

Codex is a markdown editor, so an image is just a markdown image. What Codex adds is storage: an image dropped or pasted into the editor is uploaded and kept as an asset, and the markdown points at a path Codex serves rather than at someone else's server. This keeps a universe self-contained, lets images show up in exports, and stops links from rotting. Pasting an external image URL still works when that is what you want; it simply is not stored. Public pages are stricter (#1570): the reading page, a profile bio and anything else a signed-out reader can open render author markdown with `renderPublicMarkdown`, which shows only images Codex serves (`/assets/<id>`) and turns any other image into a plain link labelled with its alt text, so a listed author cannot track readers through an image request. The EPUB build follows the same rule (`renderEpubMarkdown`, #1594): only the images bundled into the book load, and any other image becomes a link, since an edition's EPUB can be a public download. Shelf cards (Browse, the profile shelf, a universe page) show a story's description as plain text (`markdownToPlainText`), since the card is itself one link. Each story can also carry a cover image; in the library and on the public shelf a book without one gets a generated default cover built from its title on a colour the author can change, so the shelf never looks broken. Image files live on disk for self-host and in object storage for the hosted service; only their metadata is in the database. See `schema.md` for the `assets` table. Before an inline image, cover, avatar or badge upload (not the images bundled in a story import), the browser scales a still image longer than 2560 px on its long edge down to that size and re-encodes it as WebP (`src/lib/image-shrink.ts`), keeping the original when the copy is not smaller, when the browser cannot encode WebP, or when the file is a GIF or another animated image. The server accepts and checks uploads exactly as before, so this saves space and bandwidth but is not a limit.

### TODO markers

While drafting you can mark a spot to come back to. Selecting text and flagging it, or typing a line that begins `TODO:`, records a marker that shows as a highlight in the prose and as an entry in a per-story TODO list you can scan at a glance. A marker can carry a short note and be checked off when done. Because the plain-text form is just text, markers survive a markdown export; the structured form (see `schema.md`, `scene_markers`) adds the checkable list and the highlight.

## Beyond the writing surface

Later phases grew surfaces the narrative above predates. What each one is for, and what good looks like for it:

- **Review.** Async feedback on a manuscript without write access: an invited guest (or the Assistant) leaves threaded comments and suggested edits on a dedicated review view, and the author accepts, rejects, or resolves them in their own time. Suggested edits are decided one at a time from their cards, or several together from the review toolbar (`.rv-toolbar`, under the Comments panel's filter when a scene has more than one pending edit): it opens a list of the scene's pending edits, each with what it removes and adds, and accepts or rejects the ticked ones. It never decides anything without that list; a single "Accept all" button was removed on 2026-07-30 for changing more than was on screen. An accept carries a fingerprint of the replacement the page showed (`suggestionStamp`, on every accept form: a card, the guided pass, the list), and `decideSuggestion` compares it with the row under lock, inside the transaction that applies the change. If the Assistant or a connected app revised the suggestion in between, the accept is refused with "This suggestion changed. Read it again." and the card reloads, so the writer never applies text they have not read. Between rounds the author can clear the story's finished notes, or all of them, in one step; one at a time, a reviewer's note is still resolved or rejected rather than deleted. Good means a reviewer needs no account and no manual, and nothing a reviewer does can change the prose without the author's say-so.
- **Insights.** One page per universe answering "how is it going": progress against each story's target and deadline, the days written lately, activity over time, coverage of the world, and the relationship web. It reads the work rather than configuring it, so it wears the ordinary workspace shell - the universe's mode strip with no mode current and one line saying why, the page's own sections as the contents on the left, the Assistant alone in the right pane. Good means the numbers prompt the next stretch of writing rather than gamify it.
- **Goals.** A per-story target word count and deadline, an account-level daily word goal, and an optional streak, surfaced in the **Writing sessions** section of Insights. Deliberately hideable; not everyone wants scorekeeping.
- **Notifications.** An in-app bell plus an email digest, with a per-kind channel matrix in account settings. The Assistant's long-running jobs surface separately as activity cards, so progress and news do not mix. A job still running when a page loads is picked up again (its card returns, and the story's pages show its progress under the mode strip).
- **Print and page setup.** Page geometry and paragraph style set once at the account level, overridable per story, and honoured the same way by the print view, the PDF, and the EPUB. Good means what the writer proofs is what a reader downloads.
- **Import.** A document import, plus re-import of Codex's own story export, so an existing manuscript moves in without retyping. An EPUB downloaded from Archive of Our Own is recognised by its publisher field and mapped onto Codex's fields (summary, rating, chapter summaries) with the tags and author's notes as story notes; Codex never fetches from AO3 itself (#718). Entity matching stays conservative: no match creates a minimal entity, ambiguity is flagged rather than guessed.
- **Admin.** The operator console: accounts and the sign-up mode, the Assistant egress policy, usage and cost, published-edition takedown, backups, and an audit log. Sized for one operator and their friends, not a fleet. An admin can make any non-admin account a **moderator**: moderators reach only the moderation pages of the admin area (published editions, which list each story's current edition that is not taken down 50 at a time with a handle or title search (titles the moderator may not open are neither shown nor searched, see Publishing below), the report queue, Browse listings, tags and the genre, warning and fandom lists), never accounts, plans, storage or instance settings. Moderation actions record who took them and when: a takedown (`publications.removed_by`), a restore (`publications.restored_by`, `restored_at`), a removal from Browse (`stories.browse_blocked_by`) and an allow in Browse (`stories.browse_allowed_by`, `browse_allowed_at`), and the moderator who closed a report (`publication_reports.resolved_by`). A restore or an allow keeps only the latest one; there is no fuller history. Admin > Published shows the takedown, Browse removal, latest restore (on a current edition that is not taken down, and on the edition's own page) and latest allow with the moderator's name; Admin > Reports shows who closed each report. The moderation pages are routes of their own under `/admin` (`/admin/published`), gated by `requireModerator`, while every other section stays behind `requireAdmin`.

## Accounts and access

Codex assumes accounts. The same surfaces serve self-host users and hosted-instance users; only the operator differs.

**Sign-up.** An instance-wide setting controls who can create an account: closed, invite only, require approval (the default), or open. A public sign-up page captures email, password, and display name, and under the approval mode an optional "What are you working on?" note (`users.signup_note`, up to 500 characters, kept only for a request that waits in the queue). Under the approval mode the account is created but unapproved: a row with `approved_at = null`, and sign-in is blocked with a "your account is pending review" message until the operator approves; an invited or open sign-up starts approved. Email verification runs in parallel: a confirmation link is sent on sign-up, clicking it sets `email_verified_at`. Both gates have to pass before sign-in succeeds. The landing and sign-in pages read the mode, not a closed/open boolean: they offer the sign-up page under approval and open (worded "Create an account" when it is open), and leave it out under invite only and closed, because a visitor with no code cannot finish one. An invite link still reaches the sign-up page directly, and the invite-mode text links it in prose for someone who was handed a code without a link. The landing page also states which of the four is in force, in a sentence, beside the rest of the model.

**Instance type.** An instance-wide setting (`app_settings` key `instance_type`) says whether the instance is private (`personal`, the default) or a public service (`service`). It only changes the signed-out landing page: a private instance shows a short page with the title, the sign-in and sign-up buttons, and one sentence on who can get an account; a service shows the full page with the workspace figure and the model grid. It is set in Admin > Instance and is independent of the sign-up mode and of Browse.

**Approval.** A small admin page lists pending accounts with name, email, sign-up time, the note they wrote on the sign-up form if any, and approve/reject buttons. For an operator running an instance for friends, "I get a notification when someone signs up, I look at the email, I click approve" is the whole workflow. Self-rejection on the admin's part also exists for clearing out spam attempts. Invite codes are an alternative, added later (Phase 6, migration 0026): an `invite_codes` table short-circuits the approval gate when a valid code is presented on sign-up.

**Sign-in, password reset, sessions.** Standard session-based flows: a session cookie issued after sign-in, "forgot password" with email link, password reset, sign-out. Nothing custom unless something specifically needs it.

**Account self-service.** Each account has a settings page: change display name, change password, change email (with re-verification), manage two-factor methods (an authenticator app or a passkey), review and revoke active sessions, set a public handle, and set display preferences. Two destructive actions live in a separate "danger zone": export everything I own (a single archive of every universe, story, scene, entity, note, and uploaded image as markdown with front matter) and delete my account. Deletion is a hard delete with a confirmation step, not a soft flag: it removes every row and file the account owns, and on the hosted service it also purges anything that user published from the public archive. This is what lets the tool answer cleanly to a GDPR erasure request; if you want your data back, export first.

**Access for outside apps.** A writer can let an AI app they already use connect to their account (#1175). Codex is its own OAuth 2.1 authorization server for this: discovery documents under `/.well-known/`, a registration endpoint for clients that register themselves, client ID metadata documents for clients that are named by a URL, a consent page at `/oauth/authorize`, and token and revoke endpoints. Clients are public (no secrets), PKCE with S256 is required, and tokens are opaque and stored as hashes: a one-hour access token and a 30-day refresh token that is replaced on every use, where a second use of a spent one ends the connection unless it comes within a minute of the first (an app that refreshes twice at once or retries after a lost reply gets another new pair; a revoked one is never accepted). Two switches gate it: an instance switch in Admin, on by default, which makes every one of these routes answer 404 when off (the Connected apps section stays for a writer who still has apps or tokens, so they can disconnect them before access comes back), and an account switch, off by default, in the Connected apps section of the account page; turning it on asks for the password (and the authenticator code when two-factor is on), turning it off asks for nothing. A connection is a grant of one writer to one client carrying its permissions (`read` always, `suggest` by choice, `apply` by choice and never ticked for the writer even when the app asks for it, unless the existing connection already has it, #1362). The consent page sets the name apart as a claim ("It calls itself:", then the name in a box of its own, so nothing in a name can read as Codex speaking) with the host its answer goes to on a line of its own, and the Connected apps list names an app by the name it gives itself together with a host: for a client that registered itself, the host its answer is sent to (a client's redirect URIs may name one remote host at most, with any number of addresses on this computer beside it; the consent page names the host of the redirect URI in use, and the grant records the host the code was sent to, so the Connected apps list and the name on the app's notes show that host too), and for a metadata document, the host the document lives on (a document that could not be fetched or did not qualify is not asked for again for a minute, and fetches of one address at once are shared). The name itself proves nothing; control and format characters and quotation marks are stripped from it. A request to the consent page that is faulty (an unsupported response type, a missing challenge) is shown to the writer as an error with a link back to that host, not answered by a redirect; only the writer's own Deny redirects with an error. A writer disconnects one app from the list; every app is disconnected when the account switch goes off, on a password change or reset, when an admin suspends the account, and when the account is scheduled for deletion. Disconnecting one app also spends any authorization code it has not yet exchanged. Signing out other sessions leaves connections alone. Issuing (the code the consent page hands out, a refresh) holds the user row shared and revoking (those events, and a token handed back at the revoke endpoint) locks it, so a revoke is never overtaken by an issue already under way. A code exchange takes the lock too, since it may end the tokens of the app's other address. The consent page's code is also refused when the session that pressed Allow has ended in the meantime. A personal token handed back at the revoke endpoint ends its grant, as revoking it from the list does. Making a personal token takes the same exclusive lock, so its name and the limit of 20 hold for requests sent together. When a client connects again from its other address, the tokens that went to the first end, so the host a connection shows is where every live token of it was sent; the consent page says beforehand which connection will end. The endpoint opens no stream that waits for change events (`subscriptions/listen` is refused): nothing is published on one, and it would outlive its token. These routes never read the session cookie except the consent page, which needs the signed-in writer; a visitor who is sent to it signed out returns to it after signing in (the only path the sign-in return accepts), and so does a writer whose sign-in ended before they answered: the request is rebuilt from the form's own fields. The tokens are for the MCP endpoint at `/api/mcp`, which offers the Assistant's read tools to the connected app and, when the writer allowed suggestions, the tools that stage comments and suggested edits under the app's own name for the writer to decide on in Review. An app reads its own notes back with what the writer decided and answered, so it can talk an edit over before the decision, and a pattern note it leaves opens in the guided pass from a link (`?pass=<note id>` on the review page). With `apply`, the app can also show the guided pass as a card inside itself (MCP Apps, see The same toolbox over MCP in `assistant.md`), where the writer accepts, rewords or skips each edit. A writer can also make a personal access token in the same section, for a tool of their own that cannot go through the consent page: named, shown once, stored as a hash in the same token table, valid for 30, 90 (the default) or 365 days, and made only after the password is re-entered (and a code from the authenticator app when two-factor is on). It carries the same permissions, is used at the same endpoint under the same checks, attributes what it stages to its name, and is revoked from the list and by everything that disconnects apps. With the same permission an app can suggest an entry the universe is missing and propose a scene split, both of which wait under Suggestions in the universe's settings, and hand the writer a draft for a scene, which waits on the scene like a draft from the Assistant and is listed under Suggestions too. The library shows a count chip beside a universe's name while any of these wait (`countProposals`), linking to Suggestions; there is no bell notification for them. What waits for a decision is not authored content and is mostly not exported: suggested edits, suggested entries and proposed splits stay out of the account export, and what the writer accepted or kept goes out as part of the scene or entry it became. Undecided drafts are the exception (#1334): the account export writes each ready one as markdown under the story folder's `waiting-drafts/`, front matter naming its scene by path, and the story import brings them back as ready drafts on their scenes. The story export leaves them out. Review comment threads are the exception and are in the account export, an app's included (`export-reviews.ts`). A decision under Suggestions is checked against the universe of the page it was made on, inside the deciding query. Every call is scoped to the writer the token resolves to (see The same toolbox over MCP in `assistant.md`).

**Plans, entitlements, and quotas.** What a user may do is expressed through a plan and its entitlements (see `schema.md`), modelled from the start so the shape is settled before it is needed. Every user is on a plan; the default plan grants everything, which is the right setting for self-host. An admin manages plans (name, and limits on universes, stories, uploaded-image storage, and the size of one image, which can only lower the 10 MB ceiling every upload has) in Admin > Plans, assigns them per account under Usage & storage, and sees each account's use against its plan there, 50 accounts a page with a search by email or name. A create, restore from the trash, import or upload that would pass a limit is refused with a message naming the limit and telling the writer to ask the admin (a refused restore also says that moving another universe or story to the trash makes room, and that the item stays in the trash on its usual deletion date); trashed universes and stories do not count toward the count limits. Space comes back when images are deleted, which (short of deleting the account, or a failed story import removing the images it had stored) only a background sweep does, nightly. Deleting a universe for good deletes none of its images: it unlinks them from the universe and marks them unreferenced from that moment (replacing any earlier mark, which may be stale), so the sweep frees each once it has been unreferenced for 30 days and nothing shows it. The sweep works in two phases: the first run that finds an image unreferenced records the date, a run that finds it in use again clears it, and so does any write that stores its id in between (a database trigger on every table), so an image put back into use and taken out again between two runs starts its 30 days over. An image is deleted only once 30 days have passed since its mark with nothing using it in that time, never one added in the last 30 days. A replaced or removed cover, avatar or badge is left for that sweep, since another page may still show it, so it counts until then; the upload that replaces one counts the old image as freed in the limit check only, so a writer at the limit can swap and the usage reads above the limit until the sweep frees it. Only the image a swap actually replaces counts (the upload reads it under a lock on the account, story or entry), so two swaps at once cannot both count the same one; removing an image first and uploading another afterwards counts nothing as freed. Deleting a universe for good holds its owner's account for the whole delete, so that account's uploads and avatar or badge changes wait until it finishes; taking the account first is what keeps it from deadlocking with an account deletion running at the same time. Billing is deferred well beyond this: there is no payment processing and no Stripe in the foreseeable plan. A subscription layer, if it ever arrives, sets a user's `plan_id` without disturbing the rest of the model. Exploring a paid hosted tier is a deliberate someday, not a launch concern.

**Distinct from collaboration.** None of the account surfaces here support shared editing or shared ownership of content. Every universe and story has a single `owner_id`. A guest-review role lets an author invite someone (an existing user or not) to comment in threads and propose suggested edits on a single story, without that guest gaining an account or write access to the prose. See Review and collaboration in `schema.md`.

## Public reading pages

Codex assumes private writing, but a writer can choose to share finished work as a public reading page. This brings in a new kind of person: a reader, with no account, who follows a link, reads, and never opens the writing app. Their experience is deliberately separate from the author's.

**Publishing is gated.** A user cannot publish to the public web until an admin enables a public archive for their account. This gives the operator a chance to look over what someone intends to share before it is reachable at a public URL, which is the main lever for keeping material that should not be there (someone uploading a novel that is not theirs, for instance) off the instance. The gate applies to every public page, independent of adult content. The operator also keeps a takedown: an admin or a moderator can remove a published edition, which hides it without destroying the author's own copy. A takedown records who did it and a reason from the report reason list (`#lib/report-reasons`), and the author sees that reason in the story's Publish settings. Taking down a superseded edition also takes down the current one, and the author cannot publish the story again until a moderator restores the current edition (Admin > Published, "Taken down"). A restore lifts the takedown from every edition of the story it holds. A takedown always carries a reason; the editions an account deletion takes down carry none and do not hold, though a moderator can still take one down (it then holds if the deletion is cancelled): Admin > Published lists those editions among the current ones with a pill saying the account deletion hid them, and keeps their Take down button. A moderator can read an edition at `/admin/published/<edition id>` (the title links there from the report queue, and from Published when it opens), whether it is up, down or replaced, with who took it down and who last restored it. The current edition of a story that is shared (not private, not in the trash) always opens there; any other edition (an older one, or one of a story the author has made private or put in the trash) opens only while it has an open report, or one closed by a takedown that still holds on the story (an edition of the story is down with that takedown's time, `taken_down_at`, equal to the report's `resolved_at`; a takedown from before migration 0133 has no such time and does not count), so neither a dismissed report nor a lifted takedown unlocks it, and a takedown alone does not either: the current edition of a private story that a takedown of a reported older edition also put down stays unread. Admin > Published offers Take down only on an edition the moderator may read there, and the takedown itself refuses any other, since a private edition is not public. The moderation lists (Admin > Published's current editions, "Removed from Browse" and "Taken down", and Admin > Reports' recently closed reports) show an edition's title only when the moderator may read it there; otherwise the server sends no title and the row names the edition by its handle as "a story that is not shared", and the Published title search does not match it (a handle search still does), so a list never shows the title of a story its writer never shared or has taken back. The open report queue always shows titles, since an open report opens its edition. Its inline images load there too, also while it is down: the image route serves a moderator any image of the edition owner's that appears in an edition they may read on that page, uncached (#1791). Admin > Published and the overview's published-editions figure count the same editions: each story's current edition that is not taken down, including ones an account deletion hid (#1797). Nothing is restored while the author's deletion is pending, and scheduling a deletion marks every edition of the account (`publications.hidden_by_account_deletion`), so a later restore of a takedown lets the author publish again but leaves that edition down, as the cancelled deletion left their others. The restore and a publish both lock the author's account row, which scheduling a deletion updates first, so neither can overlap it, and a publish is refused while the deletion is pending.

**Genres, content warnings, tags and fandoms.** An author labels a story in its Publish settings. Genres (up to 3), content warnings (any number) and fandoms (up to 5) come from lists that admins and moderators keep under Admin > Genres, Admin > Content warnings and Admin > Fandoms; authors pick from them and cannot add to them. A migration seeds a starting set of genres and warnings, and from then on they are edited in the admin area, not in code. Content warnings describe that edition's text, so like `is_adult` their names and tag ids are frozen onto the edition at publish (`publications.content_warnings`, `content_warning_ids`); genres, tags and fandoms are live on the story and apply when saved. Free-form tags (up to 20, 50 characters each) are typed by authors; a tag's slug folds case and treats spaces, hyphens and underscores as one separator, so spellings that differ only in those share one tag, and the name shows as first typed. All four live in `tags` (`kind` tag, fandom, genre or warning) and `story_tags`. A universe can be linked to a fandom (`universes.fandom_id`, #1460): the new-universe name box suggests close matches from the list (`pg_trgm` word similarity, `/api/fandoms`), and universe settings has a Known setting field to link, change or unlink. Linking ticks `established_setting`; unlinking leaves it. Every story in a linked universe carries the fandom on its page and in Browse (`tagsForStories` adds it, marked inherited, following merges and hiding a removed fandom), and it counts toward the story's five without being stored on the story (a story already at five when its universe is linked keeps them, but cannot add more). The universe export writes it as `fandom` in universe.md, and story.md lists it among the story's `fandoms`, so a story exported alone keeps it; an import into a universe linked to that fandom does not store it again. Admins and moderators can rename, merge and remove entries on each of those admin pages (and add entries to the three lists): a merge only sets `merged_into_id` and reads follow it, so it can be undone (an author's save keeps a merged entry whose survivor they kept, and a removed entry they cannot see, so undoing a merge or a removal gives those stories the old entry back); a merge always lands on a surviving entry (naming a merged one merges into its survivor) and the source's own merged entries move with it, so no chain is more than one step deep (undoing the merge brings back only the source: the entries that had been merged into it stay on the survivor, and their stories keep showing the survivor until a moderator undoes those merges too); data from before that rule could hold a loop or a longer chain, which migration 0125 repaired once (a loop's oldest entry became its survivor, and an entry more than 8 steps from its survivor now points at it directly), and a merge that still meets one says so instead of asking to try again; a removed entry is hidden from every story and cannot be typed again, nor can a name merged into it (#1656), and it is restored before it can be merged. The story page lists genres, fandoms, tags and warnings under the byline (and on the adult gate), saying "The author added no content warnings" when there are none rather than implying the story is safe. The shelf shows each story's genres. Exports carry `adult`, `genres`, `content-warnings`, `fandoms` and `tags` in story.md, and an import puts them back by name (genres, warnings and fandoms only when this site lists them, following merges to the surviving entry and keeping the first ones in file order up to each limit; the rest are reported). The adult gate reads "I am of legal age to view this content where I live"; there are no identity checks.

**Reports.** Every public story page links "Report this story" to `/@handle/<story>/report`, a short form in the public shell: a reason from the fixed list in code (copyright, illegal content, missing or wrong content warnings or adult flag, spam, something else) and an optional comment up to 500 characters. Nothing about the reporter is kept (no account, email or address); the form is rate limited per address in memory. Reports land in `publication_reports` and in the moderation queue at Admin > Reports, where an admin or moderator takes the edition down (closing every open report on it), removes the story from Browse (closing only that report, see Browse), or dismisses the report. Each report notifies every admin and moderator through the `publication_report` notification kind, so each can turn its email off in their notification settings. Reports are not authored content: they are not exported and go with the edition.

**Built from the same tokens.** The reading pages are the outward face of the work, not a separate product: type, colour, measure and rhythm come from the token file, so all three themes work and the prose matches the editor and print preview. They are their own shell (a bar, one column, one footer) and carry no sidebars. The theme is the reader's: with no choice stored on their device the page opens in whatever their system asks for and follows it if it changes, and the theme picker in the bar overrides and remembers. Both pages name the author by their pen name, falling back to their display name; the handle stays the address in the URL and in the shelf header. One footer at the end of both pages says the work was written in Codex, who published it, and offers a link for a reader who wants to write their own.

**The shelf.** An author can claim a handle and turn on a public profile at `/@handle`. The profile is a simple shelf: an optional short bio, the author's links (http links only, marked nofollow), an optional open-for-commissions line, then the published books as a flat list, newest first, each with its cover - which, until an author uploads artwork, is the story's own title set in the reading serif. (An earlier sketch grouped the shelf by universe; the flat list is what shipped and reads better for a small shelf.) A story's reading page is at `/@handle/<story slug>`, and the shelf links it there; the id address (`/@handle/<story id>`) from before slugs keeps working. A rename moves the slug. Once the story has been published, the old slug redirects permanently (308) to the new one, for as long as the story is readable at its slug (sent with `Cache-Control: no-cache`, so a browser asks again each time and a rename back to the old slug cannot leave it looping), and the account keeps it reserved (`slug_history`), so no other story can take it and inherit the old links. A story renamed before its first publish reserves nothing, since no public link can name that slug; a universe's old slug is always reserved, because its stories' editions can be purged while the universe and its shared address live on. A purged story's old slugs stay reserved and 404. Trashing still frees a story's current slug at once, as it always has; a link to a trashed story 404s, and a new story may take that slug. Universes follow the same rule. The app's own `/stories/<slug>` and `/universes/<slug>` addresses do not redirect old slugs: only the public pages promise stable links. Both public pages carry a canonical link to the slug address (the id address for an unlisted story). An unlisted story answers only at its id: a slug comes from the title and can be guessed, which would defeat direct-link-only, so the app links an unlisted story by id everywhere (`readingAddress`). A universe with at least one listed story has a page of its own at `/@handle/universes/<universe slug>` (or its id): the universe's name and its listed stories, laid out as the shelf, and linked from the kicker of each of its stories. It shows nothing else of the universe, since universes have no frozen published copy; the hidden home of one-off stories never gets a page. A story and a universe can share a slug in one account, which is why the universe address carries the `universes/` prefix. A reader picks a story and reads it: chapters in order, scenes flowed together in the prose face, with none of the sidebars or worldbuilding around it, and with EPUB and PDF downloads when the edition carries them.

**Publishing is a snapshot, not a live view.** Making a story public does not expose whatever is currently on the editing screen. Publishing freezes the present text into a read-only edition; the author re-publishes to push a new edition, which is how a serial author releases the next chapter. Readers only ever see what was deliberately released, in-progress drafts never leak, and the reading path reads only from the frozen editions rather than the live editing tables. A frozen edition is small (markdown plus a little metadata), so it is cheap to cache and serve.

**Prose only, for now.** A public page shows a story's text and nothing else. Characters, places, and lore stay private, since they are often spoilers or unpublished invention. A public, read-only world wiki that shares selected worldbuilding, for the worldbuilder and TTRPG audiences, is an appealing later addition but is explicitly not part of the first version.

**Visibility per story.** A story is private by default. It can be made unlisted (reachable only by direct link, not shown on the shelf, useful for handing a draft to one person) or public (listed on the shelf).

**Adult content.** A story can be flagged as adult by its author. Codex does not ban it, but the reader site keeps flagged work out of default listings and shows a short confirmation before opening it, so a reader who wants to avoid it can. The flag travels with the published edition.

**Accessibility.** The reader pages are a public reading surface, so they meet WCAG 2.1 AA. That means semantic HTML with a correct heading outline and landmark regions, a keyboard-operable page with visible focus, text and controls that clear AA contrast in both themes, alt text on covers (including the generated default), a `lang` attribute on the prose, and respect for `prefers-color-scheme` and `prefers-reduced-motion`. The adult-content confirmation is part of this: it is reachable and operable by keyboard and announced to assistive technology, not a mouse-only overlay.

A few smaller rules round this out. Handles come from a reserved list so application routes (sign-in, settings, and the like) can never be claimed; changing a handle is allowed but breaks old links. Adult pages are served with a noindex instruction so search engines do not surface them. Discovery across authors is opt-in on both sides.

**Browse.** An instance-wide page at `/browse` (#855), off by default (`app_settings` key `browse`, Admin > Instance, independent of the instance type). While off it is a 404, the story setting is hidden, and the public bar and footer drop their link to it. It lists current, not taken-down editions of live, public stories whose author ticked "List in Browse" (`stories.listed_in_browse`, cleared whenever the story leaves public), whose author is not suspended or scheduled for deletion, and who has not opted out (`users.hidden_from_browse`, account settings). It is a GET form that works without JavaScript: `ILIKE` search over the edition's title, credit and description, the author's public name (the pen name when set, so a search never ties a pen name to the account name), and the universe name (not for standalone stories); one genre, one fandom, one tag (from the story page's label links); "hide stories with" content warnings, which hides a story whose live labels or frozen edition carry any of them (the edition froze the warnings' ids, so it matches the warning or any entry merged into it, also after a rename, #1596; an edition published before ids were frozen matches by the warning's name or that of any entry merged into it, #1534, so for those a name from before a rename is not matched); adult work hidden unless the reader ticks a box that carries the legal-age line. The choice is remembered in localStorage only, when the reader searches with it, and applied as soon as Browse opens (#1571): a pre-paint script in `app.html` sends a full page load to the same address with `adult=show` before anything paints, and inside the app links to Browse carry `adult=show` once the root layout has read the choice, a `beforeNavigate` in the root layout sends any other navigation there before its data loads, and the page's universal load catches the rest (going back to an older entry, after which the root layout's `afterNavigate` writes the loaded address into that entry, since SvelteKit leaves the address alone on a redirect during back or forward, #1646; that path also costs one server render without adult work). A full page load still costs one wasted server render: the server cannot read localStorage, so it renders the page without adult work, and the pre-paint redirect then loads it again, which counts twice against the Browse rate limits (#1595). Filters follow tag merges both ways. Newest edition first, 24 per page, keyset on `(published_at, id)`. No popularity, counts or reader tracking. Search is rate limited per IP, and every page load has a looser limit of its own. A tag filter only resolves for a tag some listed story carries, so a tag used only on private stories is not revealed. Search engines may index the plain page and single-filter pages that have results (with a canonical link to the surviving entry's slug); searches, filter combinations, adult results and later pages carry noindex. Results use `ShelfGrid` with a byline (author and universe links). A text search also lists up to six matching writers with listed stories, counting only the stories the other filters let through (genre, fandom, tag, hidden warnings and the adult choice, #1536), and the heading names every genre, fandom and tag filter that is set. Moderators can "Remove from Browse" from Admin > Published and the report queue: it clears the listing and sets `stories.browse_blocked_at` with a report reason, which the author sees in story settings and which stops them listing it again until a moderator allows it (Admin > Published lists every blocked story under "Removed from Browse"); the edition stays up. When taken from the report queue, the report acted on closes as `removed_from_browse`; other reports on the story stay open, since its pages are still up. The tag box suggests tags used on listed stories only. Similar stories (#1444): while Browse is on, a reading page (past the adult gate) ends with up to six listed stories, `ShelfGrid` with bylines, scored in one query (`similarStories` in `browse.ts`, reusing `listedInBrowseCondition`): each shared free-form tag adds ln(listed stories / listed stories with the tag), each shared genre a fixed 0.15, following merges; fandoms and warnings do not count. A match needs at least one shared tag and a score above 0.5, so genres alone never qualify. Ties go to the newer edition. The story itself need not be listed. Adult work is included on a confirmed adult story; for a reader with the remembered Browse adult choice the page fetches `/@handle/<story>/similar?adult=show` after load (rate limited per address), since the server cannot read it. No cache: each page load runs the query, which gathers every listed story once (to count them for the tag weights and to keep matches to listed stories) and reads the labels of only the stories that share one, through `story_tags_tag`. Not built: universes as Browse entries (#1445), an author directory, follows, likes, RSS, prose search, a language filter (waits for #1343), sitemaps.

The instance serves these `/@handle` pages directly for its own users, the same way whether self-hosted or hosted. They render only the frozen, published editions, never a draft, and handles are unique within the instance. (A central cross-instance handle registry would only matter if there were ever many separate instances sharing one public namespace, which the single-instance model does not need; see `roadmap.md`.)

## AI: the Assistant

Codex shipped v1 with no AI features, deliberately: the editor and the planning surface had to be good on their own merits first, and the prose corpus that emerged from real use became the calibration material for the assistance that followed. That assistance, the Assistant, has since shipped as Phase 9. `assistant.md` is the full design; this is the shape:

An opt-in Assistant, off by default and dark until the writer configures an endpoint and turns it on. Enablement is per account (a master toggle; while it is off Codex never contacts a model) with a per-story mute layered on top: a story can silence the Assistant but never enable what the account has not. The roles span three surfaces. A rubber-duck chat and grounded world Q&A live in the right sidebar's **Assistant** tab, with slash commands for drafting and copyedit passes; inline **co-author** and **continuation** (ghost-text at the cursor, Tab to accept) live in the editor; and a **reviewer** role leaves threaded comments and suggested edits through the same review framework a human guest uses. Around the roles sit smaller graces: scene-split proposals, recaps, scene summaries, and entity enrichment. Everything the Assistant writes is a suggestion the author accepts or rejects, never a silent change, and its long-running jobs surface as activity cards in the corner, distinct from notifications.

**BYO key.** Codex ships no bundled AI. Users configure their own endpoint - a preset provider or any OpenAI-compatible URL - with the key encrypted at rest, never re-shown by the UI, and per-role model selection with cost display. The operator's lever is the egress policy: block private networks (the default), an allowlist, or open, enforced server-side with a DNS-validating guard. There is no separate instance-wide Assistant switch; an allowlist with no reachable hosts is the shut-off.

Two things to keep separate: **entity autocomplete** (the ghost-text that completes known names) is local and lookup-based, has nothing to do with an LLM, and works with the Assistant fully off. **Continuation** is the LLM role. The two now coexist in the editor and share a UX pattern, not a backend.

## What Codex is not

- **Not a chat tool.** The Assistant's rubber-duck role is a writing aid, not a general-purpose chat. Users wanting a chat interface have better options.
- **Not a role-play tool.** Character dialogue belongs in the prose; no UI or schema supports character impersonation.
- **Not a book-distribution platform.** Codex can publish reading pages to the web (see Public reading pages), and a story exports as a markdown archive or an EPUB, with published editions also carrying a page-setup-faithful PDF. It does not push your book into stores; DOCX with manuscript standards remains a someday-maybe, and the markdown archive stays the canonical full-fidelity export.
- **Not a real-time collaboration tool.** Single-author-per-session is assumed. Review is async, not live: the guest-review role lets an invited reviewer leave threaded comments and suggested edits that the author resolves in their own time. There is no simultaneous multi-author editing.
- **Not a feature-gated SaaS.** The hosted instance runs the same code anyone can install themselves, under the same MIT licence. There is no paid edition with extra features; if a difference between self-host and hosted ever appears, it will be operational (someone else runs the box) rather than functional (you get more features by paying).
- **Not a mobile editing app.** Phones are too small to draft on meaningfully. Tablets in desktop mode work for review but aren't prioritised for authoring in v1. Narrow or zoomed windows stay usable: at 960px and narrower the editor and the review page show one pane at a time. Plan, Notes and Insights keep their columns and scroll sideways.

## Technical shape

- **Backend and UI:** SvelteKit on Node (current LTS), TypeScript across the whole app. Server-rendered pages and server endpoints live in one project, with a compiler-enforced split between server-only code (database access, secrets, session checks) and what ships to the browser. There is no separate frontend framework and no separate API service to run.
- **Database:** Postgres 18 with the pgvector extension (`pgvector/pgvector:0.8.7-pg18-trixie`, required from v4.0.0; the Debian base is pinned because a new one can change the text sort order and leave indexes out of order). Content is stored as markdown in `text` columns; metadata in typed columns and `jsonb` where the shape is fluid.
- **Data layer:** Drizzle. Tables are defined in TypeScript close to the SQL in `schema.md`, and migrations are generated from those definitions. The queries stay thin and SQL-first, which keeps the database driver swappable (see the desktop build note in `roadmap.md`).
- **Lock order:** write paths that lock more than one kind of row take a story's scenes, then the story, then entities, then its chapters, each lowest id first (`src/lib/server/row-locks.ts`; `lockStoryScenes` takes an advisory lock on the story before its scene rows, so a change that waited also locks a scene added meanwhile), so two of them cannot deadlock. `tests/integration/lock-order.test.ts` checks the main scene, chapter and story write paths against it (row locks only; advisory locks are checked by hand).
- **Editor:** CodeMirror 6, running in the browser. The editor's input loop is client-side; the server sees debounced saves posted to an endpoint, not keystrokes.
- **Background work:** a separate worker process handles jobs that do not belong in a request: mention-index rebuilds, transactional email, exports, backups, and the publication sync described under Public reading pages. The job queue runs on Postgres itself (pg-boss), so there is no extra infrastructure to run.
- **One clock:** every stored timestamp that is later compared with another (`updated_at`, `touched_at`, watermarks, generated-at and reviewed-at stamps) comes from the database clock: `defaultNow()` on insert, `clock_timestamp()` in `$onUpdate`, `now()` in a write, or `dbNow` / `dbNowText` from `src/lib/server/db-clock.ts` when the value is needed in code. A stamp compared at full precision is compared in SQL, since a JS `Date` keeps only milliseconds. The app clock is for display, logs, and expiry windows. ESLint refuses `new Date()` written directly in a `.set`, `.values`, `$onUpdate` or `sql` template under `src/lib/server`.
- **Auth:** server-side sessions keyed by a cookie, with passwords hashed using a vetted algorithm (argon2 or bcrypt). Sessions are revocable, so "log out everywhere" and admin revocation work, and two extra factors (an authenticator app for TOTP, and passkeys) are modelled from the start. A sign-in link by email is a secondary route on the sign-in page, beside the password and the passkey: `/login/link` mails a single-use `sign_in` token good for 15 minutes, only to an account that could sign in with its password, and using it replaces the password step only, so the two-factor step still follows. The link page peeks on GET and spends the token on the POSTed confirm; asking for a link and using one are rate limited like a password reset and a password attempt. The `users` table carries the role, approval, and email-verification columns the flows need, and an admin approval gate stands between a new account and an active one.
- **Cross-site form check:** done in `hooks.server.ts` (`src/lib/server/csrf.ts`) by SvelteKit's own rule, with the built-in check switched off in `vite.config.ts`, because the OAuth token and revoke endpoints must accept form posts from other programs and the built-in check has no per-route exception. Those two paths read no cookie, and neither does `/api/mcp`, which is left out too so that a bodyless DELETE or POST from an app gets the transport's own answer. The rule differs from SvelteKit's in one way: a request with no Content-Type counts as a form.
- **Testing:** three layers. Unit tests (Vitest) cover pure logic such as mention detection and markdown round-trip. Integration tests run against a real throwaway Postgres rather than a mock, since the data layer is thin and SQL-first and a mock would prove nothing. A small Playwright suite guards the critical journeys (sign in, create a story, draft a scene). Tests are written alongside the code, close to test-driven without being strict about it.
- **Deployment:** Docker Compose with four services: the SvelteKit app, the worker, Postgres, and Caddy as the reverse proxy for TLS. The same image runs on a home NUC for self-hosters and on the maintainer's infrastructure for the hosted service.
- **Licence:** MIT. The hosted service is offered as a convenience for users who do not want to run Docker, not as a paid edition. Anyone can take the source and run their own instance, public or private. (Modelled on Ghost rather than GitLab: same code in both places, no Community/Enterprise split.)

The stack is chosen to stay easy to maintain over a long-running project and approachable to anyone who helps later. One language across the whole app keeps the context small, and the editor, the hardest part, is native to that language rather than reached through an interop layer. The pieces are deliberately the settled, boring ones rather than the newest, since low churn matters more here than novelty. Heavier orchestration and microservices were considered and rejected as overkill for a four-service application.

### Hosting and tenancy

The application is single-operator and multi-user: one person runs an instance for themselves and any friends they invite, each of whom owns their own universes and stories. That is the self-host shape, and the hosted service is the same shape - the maintainer runs one instance and opens sign-up on it.

There is one instance and one database, shared by everyone on it, in the GitHub model: your work is scoped to you and private by default, and the pages you choose to publish are reachable by anyone. There is no fleet, no instance-per-user, and no control plane to provision or route tenants; the hosted service is simply this one application running, with accounts on it. Self-host and hosted run identical code.

Because the instance is shared, isolation is a property of the code rather than the deployment: every universe and story carries a single `owner_id`, and every query that reads or writes a user's private content is scoped to the signed-in owner. One user's unpublished work is kept out of another's reach by that scoping, so it has to hold on every path - which makes a deliberate cross-tenant isolation audit part of preparing the instance for sign-ups (see `roadmap.md`, Phase 5). The published `/@handle` reader pages are the only content that crosses between users, and only ever the frozen editions a user chose to make public.

Portability is achieved through export, not through storage format. The database is the running format; markdown archives and EPUB are the portable artefacts produced on demand. Version history is a polymorphic `revisions` table rather than Git.

## Open questions

- **Editing in the continuous view.** Resolved: shipped as stitched per-scene editors (step 23b). Scene marks inside the flow are now a preference, account-level with a per-story override, defaulting to shown.
- **Plotlines or arcs as first-class entities.** Currently modelled through scene tags and metadata `jsonb`. May be promoted to their own table if usage shows it's needed.
- **Command palette (Ctrl+K).** Resolved: shipped in Phase 7, absorbing search and cross-view navigation into one surface.
- **Assistant tab refinements.** Resolved: the chat tab keeps any number of chats per story or universe, with a picker to switch between them and search them (issue #985). See `assistant.md`.
- **Entity colours with meaning.** Resolved: shipped in v1.2. Characters and places can join a universe-defined category that carries a colour (nullable `category_id`), grouping the cast by whatever meaning the author gives it (factions, families, POV tiers). Per-entity badge colours and images followed later (issue #305).
- **How much markdown the editor should show.** Resolved: an `editingMode` preference (markdown or rich) shipped in Phase 7, where rich is CodeMirror live-preview (syntax marks hide except on the line being edited). The default flipped to rich in v2.25.0. The formatting toolbar and the shared renderer (exports, reading pages) shipped alongside.
- **Preference layering.** Resolved: shipped in Phase 7 (migration 0032). `stories.preferences` jsonb holds per-story overrides merged over the user's `users.preferences` at load time, for the editor-behaviour keys; "use my account setting" clears the override.
- **Sidebar resize.** Resolved: the side columns grow with the window between fixed bounds (`--left-w` 264 to 320px, `--right-w` 312 to 380px, issue #733), and the writer can drag either inner edge to set a width (issue #984), kept per browser.
- **How aggressive revision pruning should be.** Consecutive autosaves within two minutes coalesce into one row, and nothing is pruned. Thinning old autosaves was built and dropped (issue #599): at about 7 KB per row it saves roughly 20 MB a year for a heavy writer, which is not worth deleting drafts. Revisit for the hosted service with measured table sizes.
- **Guest review (reviewer role).** Resolved: shipped in Phase 6 - comments first (v2.8.0), then suggested edits (v2.9.0), with magic-link guest identity and the accept/reject UI.
